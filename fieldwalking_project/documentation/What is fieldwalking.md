# What is Fieldwalking? A Guide to the Method

## The short version

Fieldwalking is a systematic survey technique where people walk across a field in an organised pattern, recording and collecting archaeological material visible on the ground surface — mostly pottery, flint, and other artefacts that have been brought up by ploughing. It's one of the cheapest and least invasive ways to find out what's buried beneath a landscape without digging a single hole.

## Why it works

Ploughing repeatedly drags buried material upward and spreads it across the topsoil. Over decades, this creates a scatter of artefacts on the surface that roughly reflects what's underneath — a dense pottery scatter often marks a settlement site; a scatter of struck flint might indicate prehistoric activity; a scatter of Roman tile could mark a building. Fieldwalking exploits this "ploughsoil signature" to map where past human activity may have been concentrated.

## How it's actually done

* **Transects**: The field is divided into parallel walking lines, typically spaced at a fixed interval (varies by project).
* **Walking speed and eye line**: Walkers move at a steady pace, scanning a set width either side of the line, picking up everything of archaeological interest within that strip.
* **Bagging**: Each find goes into a separately labelled bag with a unique identification number (finds number), so the spatial location of each find can be accurately logged.



## What gets recorded, not just collected

Good fieldwalking isn't just "pick up the pottery" — the *absence* of finds in a transect is just as informative as their presence, so a proper methodology records:

* Ground conditions (crop cover, soil moisture, visibility) for every campaign
* Weather and light conditions on the day
* Who walked which transect, since individual finders vary in detection rate
* GPS or grid coordinates for each collection unit

This kind of contextual detail is often called **paradata** — data about how the data was collected — and it matters because visibility conditions can drastically skew what gets found. A scatter that looks "dense" might just be a stint walked on a good day by a sharp-eyed volunteer, not a genuinely richer part of the site.

## What you get at the end

The output is usually a distribution map: findspots  plotted spatially,  colour-coded by material type. Dense clusters can suggest settlement foci; thin, even scatters across a field might just reflect manuring practice (spreading domestic waste, including old pottery, onto fields as fertiliser) rather than occupation at all — a classic interpretive trap for fieldwalking data.

## Strengths and limitations

**Strengths:**

* Non-destructive — nothing is dug up or disturbed beyond what's already on the surface
* Cheap relative to geophysics or trial trenching
* Covers large areas quickly 
* Good at picking up scatters that geophysics might miss (geophysics is poor at detecting small, discrete artefact scatters with no buried cut features)

**Limitations:**

* Only works on ploughed, arable land — useless under pasture, woodland, or built-up areas
* Biased by visibility conditions, walker experience, and even the time of year
* Can't detect anything that's never reached the surface, or sites without a strong artefact signature (e.g. some earthwork or timber sites)
* Manuring scatters and genuine occupation scatters can look similar without further interpretation

## Where it fits in the wider toolkit

Fieldwalking is typically one stage in a broader, non-intrusive assessment alongside desk-based research, aerial photography, LiDAR, and geophysical survey — each technique picks up a different kind of evidence, and used together they build a much stronger picture than any one method alone before a decision is made on whether trial trenching is needed.
